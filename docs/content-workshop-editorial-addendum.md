**How to use this:** These continue the style guidance in the Content Workshop Blog Writer project instructions (`Content_Workshop_Writer_Agent.md`). Each rule cites the specific reviewer feedback it came from, so a future editor — human or AI — can see *why* the rule exists, not just what it says. Rules that hold up across two or more independent pieces get folded into the main instructions file under the relevant section; until then, they live here. **This file is dated at the top on every update** so anyone consulting it — including a new AI assistant with no memory of prior rounds — knows how current the guidance is. **Editors (internal or contractor) who need the short version should use the Editor Checklist directly below**; everything after it is the sourcing.

**Caveat:** Round 1 was a single review, one piece — a few of its rules were promoted immediately anyway because they were countable mechanical or factual checks, not taste calls. Rounds 2–6 add five more pieces. Round 4 drew comments from JB Clark and David Ebner (Christina Rowell did not comment on that piece); Round 5 drew four margin comments and roughly twenty tracked changes from JB Clark only; Round 6 drew ten margin comments and about eight tracked changes from Susan Ishmael, a new reviewer. (The Drive export doesn't show who made the Round 6 tracked changes. They line up with Susan's comments, so this file attributes them to her.) **The two-instance bar**: a subjective or stylistic note needs to recur independently in two or more pieces (or two or more rounds) before it hardens into a rule. A factual or mechanical note — spelling, a broken structure, an explicit "hardcode this" instruction from a reviewer, a terminology decision — can promote on one instance. A new reviewer flagging a pattern an earlier reviewer already flagged counts as strong independent confirmation, because she had not seen the earlier notes. Items clear the bar when the same pattern shows up again, independently, in a later round; those promotions are marked below.

## Editor Checklist [NEW, ROUND 6]

*Requested by Susan Ishmael, Round 6: "Let's actually do this, and create a true checklist for editors whether it's an internal editor or a contractor." Built from the confirmed rules, mechanics, and high-confidence Watch List items below. Run the passes in order. Each pass looks for one thing.*

### Pass 1: Facts and names
- [ ] Core keyword phrase appears in the exact word order given, and the exact-match phrase is used once. *(Rule 1)*
- [ ] Every company, tool, product, program, and internal asset name matches its source of record (the company's site, Notion, a prior approved piece). *(Rule 2)*
- [ ] Every statistic and citation can be traced to a source on request. *(Mechanics)*
- [ ] Any sentence that promises a number of items ("three reasons") is followed by that many items. *(Rule 3)*
- [ ] Every number that stands in for a noun names the noun ("three sites," not "three"). *(Mechanics)*

### Pass 2: Count against the limits
| What to count | Limit |
| :- | :- |
| "Not X, it's Y" contrasts, in either order, in one sentence or across two. This includes "instead of X, Y." | 0 in titles and headings; 1 in body copy |
| Mirrored role pairs ("X earns A. Y earns B.") | 0. Rewrite any you find. |
| "Earn" in any form | 1 per piece |
| Em dashes | 3 per blog, set closed (`word—word`); 0 in LinkedIn posts and emails |
| Humor, irreverence, or a recognizable everyday reference | At least 1, usually in the first H2 |

*(Rules 4 and 10; Watch List; Mechanics)*

### Pass 3: Find and replace words
Search for each word or phrase. Check every hit.
- [ ] **reviewer** → **editor** in reader-facing copy. *(Rule 12)*
- [ ] **ship / ships / shipped** → "publish" or "hit publish." *(Watch List)*
- [ ] **ceiling** → "limit," or rephrase. *(Watch List)*
- [ ] **argument** → "message," "point," or "the piece." *(Watch List)*
- [ ] **silent / quiet** as modifiers → rewrite. *(Watch List)*
- [ ] "The distinction matters more than it sounds like it should," plus variants ("this matters more than it seems"). Cut. *(Rule 6)*
- [ ] "Let's dive in," "let's delve in," "let's explore," "in today's fast-paced world." Cut. *(Main guide)*
- [ ] "Isn't just / doesn't just." Rewrite all but one at most. *(Main guide)*
- [ ] "Most teams / most marketers get this wrong." Describe the mistake, not the people who make it. *(Watch List)*

### Pass 4: Read for the sentence-level tells
- [ ] **Short punchline sentences.** Look at every short sentence that closes a paragraph or follows a heading. If it states a slogan, an abstraction, or a metaphor the reader has to decode, replace it with a plain statement of who does what, or cut it. *(Rule 11)*
- [ ] **Repeated points.** For each section, name the one point it makes. Cut any sentence that repeats a point already made, including a sentence that restates its own heading. Look hardest at the middle third. *(Rule 9)*
- [ ] **Figurative language.** Would the reader get the business consequence without decoding the image? No mixed metaphors. *(Main guide; Base Rule Confirmations)*
- [ ] **Throat-clearing.** Cut sentences that announce a point is coming instead of making it. *(Rule 6)*
- [ ] **Point of view.** "You" stays "you" inside a section. Content Workshop never speaks as if it works inside the reader's company. *(Imported Candidate; Rule 10)*
- [ ] **Audience respect.** Nothing implies that the reader, especially a technical buyer, lacks understanding. *(Main guide)*

### Pass 5: Structure
- [ ] Intro paragraphs are one or two sentences each, and imperative openers give each instruction its own sentence. *(Watch List)*
- [ ] List items that need a full sentence become an H3 plus a paragraph. Bullets are kept for fragments. *(Rule 7)*
- [ ] No H2 is devoted to exceptions or counterarguments. A boundary condition gets one or two sentences inside the relevant section. *(Watch List)*
- [ ] Jokes make the same point as the paragraph they sit in, don't interrupt a cause-and-effect sequence, use a current reference, and are never at the reader's expense. *(Rule 10)*
- [ ] Title, H1, and meta title get a shortening pass last, after the body is settled. *(Rule 5)*

### Pass 6: Final
- [ ] Every suggested edit from another editor is applied for its intent, and then the new wording is run back through Passes 2–4. Suggested wording can break the limits too. *(Process Notes)*
- [ ] Proofread again after every rewrite pass. *(Mechanics)*
- [ ] Word count lands on target without filler. *(Main guide)*
- [ ] Images: primary 1860 x 930 px, inline 860 x 310 px, brand colors #fbd139, #0e213d, #0066ff. *(Main guide)*

## Confirmed Rules (Rounds 1–6)

### 1. Match the user's keyword word order exactly, and use the exact-match phrase once.
*(Round 1 — unchanged. See prior version of this addendum for full sourcing.)*

### 2. Verify names against the source — including internal asset and product names, not just external company names.

The original Round 1 rule covered third-party company names. Round 2 surfaces the same underlying failure mode applied internally: a piece referenced the "**Quick Wins Playbook**" as an established asset name, and two reviewers weren't sure that was actually right.

> JB Clark: *"Is this what we're calling it?"* Christina Rowell, same anchor: *"I thought that's what it was called in notion?"* David Ebner extended the thought further, asking whether the Playbook's contents could be mirrored from Notion to the site as a teaser.

Neither reviewer contradicted the name — they questioned whether it matched the canonical source (Notion, in this case) with enough confidence to publish it. **Amended scope:** before a piece ships, verify not just external company/tool names but internal product, program, and asset names against whatever system of record defines them (Notion, the internal wiki, a previous approved piece) — the same discipline Rule 2 already requires for a company like Ahrefs applies to a proprietary asset like a playbook or community name.

### 3. When a sentence promises a specific count, deliver an actual list.
*(Round 1 — unchanged.)*

### 4. Ration — and now strictly police — the negative-setup / positive-insertion construction. [REINFORCED, ROUND 4; HOLDING, ROUNDS 5–6]

Round 1 named this the "X is not the thing — Y is" antithesis and set a working ceiling of two per piece. Rounds 2 and 3 showed the construction surviving that ceiling by changing shape rather than frequency (splitting across two sentences), which prompted a revised ceiling: **zero in titles/headings, no more than one in body copy per piece.**

**Round 4 shows the revised ceiling is still not holding at the drafting stage.** The Missing Circle piece shipped a draft with three instances of the construction in body copy, not one:

1. *"It's not that you don't have ideas, it's that they seem to be missing something."*
2. *"That's a sourcing problem, not a creativity problem."* — flagged directly: JB Clark: *"Too many of these."*
3. *"Not 'we have a product in this category,' but 'we've seen this pattern enough times to have a real opinion about it.'"*

JB Clark's comment landed on instance #2, but the phrasing — "too many of *these*," plural, not "cut this line" — indicates he was flagging the pattern's density across the draft, not one sentence in isolation. This is the same failure mode Round 3 diagnosed (a rule that measures one surface form gets satisfied while the underlying tic keeps shipping), except this time the ceiling itself was already explicit and specific, and the draft still cleared it three times over.

**Diagnosis:** the ceiling is not the problem. The problem is that nothing forces a check against the ceiling before the draft goes to review. A one-per-piece cap that lives only in this addendum doesn't get enforced unless someone counts.

**Amended rule, effective immediately:** before a draft is finalized, run an explicit find-pass for the construction (any sentence or sentence pair that states what something is not, then what it is, in either order) and cut down to the ceiling — zero in titles/headings, one maximum in body copy — as a required drafting step, not a hoped-for outcome. If more than one instance survives to review, treat it as a process miss, not a style nitpick.

**Round 5 update: the ceiling held for the first time.** The AI positioning draft contained exactly one instance in body copy (*"buyers don't purchase a feature list; they purchase what changes after the contract is signed"*), zero in headings, and no reviewer flagged the construction. However, JB Clark flagged a sibling construction in the same family (mirrored sentence pairs that assign roles: "X earns A. Y earns B."). See the Watch List. Until that item resolves, extend this rule's find-pass to catch it too.

**Round 6 update: no reviewer flag, but watch the "instead of" variant.** The AI content editing piece quotes the construction twice as an example of a tell ("It's not X, it's Y"; "It's not about volume, it's about relevance"), and nobody objected. **Decision:** a quoted example used to illustrate the tell does not count toward the limit. The new risk came from the reviewer side. Both of Susan Ishmael's suggested replacement sentences use the "instead of X, Y" form (*"instead of asking your editors to work harder, create a better system..."* and *"Instead of copyediting for misspellings, cliches, and wonky sentence structure, today's editors must stretch their scope..."*). That is the same contrast in a different shape. Taking both verbatim would put the draft over the limit. **Amended scope:** the find-pass includes "instead of X, Y" and "rather than X, Y" when they set up a contrast.

### 5. Titles and headline-length assets get a deliberate shortening pass before they ship.

David Ebner asked for a shorter title in two independent pieces, a full round apart:

> Round 2, on "The AI Marketing Course for Marketers Who Don't Have Time for Another AI Course": *"Maybe: AI Marketing Course for Marketers in a Hurry? We need something a bit shorter, if possible."* Round 3, on the meta title "Why Your AI Marketing Content Needs a Feedback System, Not Just a Style Guide": *"Can we shorten this?"*

Two independent instances, same reviewer, same note, two different pieces — this clears the bar. **Rule:** before finalizing a title, H1, or meta title, do an explicit shortening pass — if the title stacks a main clause plus a qualifying clause (as both flagged examples did), test whether the qualifier can be cut, moved to a subtitle, or dropped entirely without losing the hook. *(Round 5: the single-clause title "AI Positioning Has a Headline Problem" drew no title note. Round 6: "AI Content Editing That Keeps the Tells Out" and the meta title "AI Content Editing: How to Fix AI Tells" drew no title note.)*

### 6. Cut the boilerplate hedge "the distinction matters more than it sounds like it should" — and treat it as one instance of a broader throat-clearing pattern to watch for.

This is the single strongest piece of evidence from Round 3: **the identical sentence** — not a paraphrase, the same eleven words — was drafted into both the Round 2 and Round 3 pieces, and cut both times in tracked changes.

> Round 2: *"[[The distinction matters more than it sounds like it should.]] A video can explain the eight-part prompt framework in ten minutes..."* Round 3: *"[[That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should.]] A model that only sees 'before' and 'after' text has no way to generalize..."*

Neither cut carries a reviewer comment explaining it, but the fact that the exact same filler sentence was generated twice and removed twice, without any reviewer needing to ask twice, means it's not incidental. **Rule:** add "the distinction matters more than it sounds like it should" (and close variants — "this matters more than it seems," "that's a bigger deal than it looks like") to the banned-clichés list in the main style guide. More broadly, this is a *transitional hedge that announces a point is about to be made instead of just making it* — the same failure mode as the already-banned "In today's fast-paced world" openers, just relocated mid-paragraph instead of at the top. *(Round 5: see the Watch List item on section-level hedging, which is the structural version of this sentence-level problem. Round 6: "Notes become rules at different speeds" was cut as a vague lead-in to the paragraph's real first sentence. See Rule 11.)*

### 7. Bulleted lists made of full sentences should become H3 + paragraph pairs, not bullets.

All three reviewers converged on the same fix, independently, in the same comment thread on the Round 3 piece's two large list sections:

> JB Clark: *"Two sections that have big lists with full sentences. We should cut down one of them a little and maybe simplify both?"* David Ebner: *"Agreed. These may be small paragraphs instead of bullets or shortened up. It's great info, though. Maybe an article all on its own!"* Christina Rowell: *"I like the idea of paragraphs. Maybe we do an H3 where the bold is the H3 and the copy under is the paragraph below."*

**Rule:** when a list item requires a full sentence (or more) to land — as opposed to a short noun phrase or fragment — restructure it as an H3 (the bolded lead-in) followed by a paragraph, rather than a bulleted list. Reserve true bullet/numbered lists for items that work as fragments. *(Note: the Round 4 piece's "What Happens When One Circle Is Missing" section already applies this pattern correctly — bolded lead-in phrase plus paragraph, no bullets — with no reviewer correction needed. First clean confirmation that the rule is sticking. Round 5's Outcome / Proof / Method section used H3 + paragraph pairs with no structural correction: second consecutive clean confirmation. Round 6: third clean confirmation on the H3 side, and the reverse case also showed up. Susan's tracked changes turned an inline series of three fragments into a numbered list. See the Watch List.)*

### 8. Conceptual frameworks need a name with personality, not just a functional label. [NEW, ROUND 4 — see Watch List; logged here as high-confidence pending a second instance]

On the piece's central framework — three overlapping circles (Timely, Painful, Yours) — JB Clark: *"I like this framing but I want it to be a little more fun, like the circle of truth, interest circle. I don't know."* David Ebner: *"Agreed. I'm down for whatever you settle on."*

Both reviewers liked the underlying mechanic; the note is specifically about the label. This is placed in the Watch List below rather than the numbered rules because it's one instance on one piece — but it's flagged as high-confidence because it's a direct, converged note from both reviewers, not a single stray comment, and it names a real gap: functional labels ("The Three Circles Framework") describe the tool but don't make it memorable or ownable the way a named framework should be. Promotes to a full rule on the next framework-naming instance.

*(Round 5: the piece's Outcome / Proof / Method framework drew no naming note, so this stays pending. The broader "more fun" preference behind it did recur, and has promoted separately as Rule 10. Round 6: the piece's three tell types, Structural, Word-Level, and Mechanical, are a descriptive grouping rather than a named framework, and drew no naming note. Still pending.)*

### 9. Cut restatements: any sentence that repeats a point the surrounding copy already makes. [PROMOTED, ROUND 5; REINFORCED, ROUND 6]

Logged on the Watch List in Round 2 with one instance. Round 5 supplies the second independent instance, plus two reviewer comments pointing at the same problem.

> Round 2, cut in tracked changes with no comment: *"[[We already ruled out the AI builders above. Here's who's left.]]"*
>
> Round 5, cut in tracked changes with no comment: *"[[Leading with AI is the new leading with features.]]"* The next paragraph already makes the same point in full (*"AI-led messaging is feature-led messaging with a bigger budget"*).
>
> Round 5, JB Clark, on the Method section and the message-map paragraph that follows it: *"Could cut some of this, get's a little repetitive."* The message-map paragraph re-explains that outcomes take the headline and AI belongs lower down, a point the intro, the H2 opener, and the Outcome section had each already made.
>
> Round 5, JB Clark, general comment on the draft: *"Feels wordy on the back half. Or maybe the middle actually."*
>
> Round 6, Susan Ishmael, on *"For those, a ceiling holds better than a ban."*: *"I'd eliminate this sentence."* The H3 directly above it already says the same thing: "Set Limits Where a Ban Won't Hold."

**Rule:** after drafting, run a restatement pass. For each section, name the one point it makes, then cut any sentence that re-states a point already made earlier in that section or in a prior one. **This includes a sentence that restates its own heading** (Round 6). Cut slogan-style summary lines ("X is the new Y") when the surrounding paragraph makes the same point in full. A thesis may be restated once, in the conclusion, on purpose. Give the middle third of the draft the hardest look, since that is where Round 5's wordiness notes landed. Round 6's flagged instance also sat in the middle third.

### 10. Every piece needs personality: at least one moment of humor, irreverence, or recognizable everyday reference. [PROMOTED, ROUND 5; REINFORCED BY A SECOND REVIEWER, ROUND 6]

Two consecutive rounds, same reviewer, same direction: the drafts read as too buttoned-up.

> Round 4, JB Clark, on the framework name: *"I want it to be a little more fun."*
>
> Round 5, JB Clark added humor himself in tracked changes, twice in the first H2:
> - *"Leading with AI was a rational decision, especially with all of our managers shouting, 'MORE AI!'"*
> - *"It's like when your local bank catches on to a TikTok trend. That's when you know it's time to pick a new trend. At this point, it's the equivalent of picking your local mortgage lender based on which bank tellers do a better Harlem Shake."*
>
> Round 6, Susan Ishmael's suggested replacement for a flat line: *"Editorial checklists help your editors see patterns in AI writing that weren't there in 'the before times.' Instead of copyediting for misspellings, cliches, and wonky sentence structure, today's editors must stretch their scope to combat AI slop."*

Round 4's note was about a framework label, and Round 5's edits were about body copy, but the underlying preference is the same and it surfaced independently in two pieces. That clears the bar. (Rule 8's narrower framework-naming item still needs its own second instance.)

**Round 6:** the draft's built-in joke (AI tells as *"the copy version of the stock photo where four colleagues high-five over a laptop"*) drew no correction, so the rule held at drafting. A second, independent reviewer then added more personality on her own: "the before times," "wonky," "AI slop." That confirms the preference isn't specific to JB. It also shows where the humor goes: Susan used it to replace a dry aphorism (see Rule 11), not to decorate a working sentence.

**Rule:** every draft includes at least one moment of humor, irreverence, or recognizable everyday or pop-culture reference, usually in the first H2 where the problem is set up. Acknowledging the pressure the reader faces from leadership ("MORE AI!") is a strong, proven source of it. Casual, current phrasing ("AI slop," "the before times") is welcome in body copy.

**Drafting guardrails** *(editor's additions based on how the Round 5 insertions read in context, not reviewer feedback; revise if reviewers disagree)*:
- The joke has to make the same point as the paragraph it sits in. The mortgage-lender half of the TikTok analogy implies buyers *do* choose vendors on surface trends, which works against the paragraph's claim that the "AI-powered" label no longer influences them.
- Place it where it doesn't interrupt a cause-and-effect sequence. The TikTok lines sit between "the claim stops carrying information" and "Buyers can't use it to choose between vendors."
- Hold point of view steady. "Our managers" puts the writer inside the reader's company; Content Workshop is the agency. "Your board," "every board deck," or "the CEO" carries the same joke without the shift. (Connects to the Imported Candidate "Hold point of view steady within a section.")
- Prefer current references to dated ones. The Harlem Shake peaked in 2013.
- One analogy per idea. The same section already had the "seatbelt in a car ad" comparison for the same point.
- Humor never comes at the reader's expense (see the persona rule and the "most teams" Watch List item).

### 11. Cut or ground the aphoristic one-liner. [NEW, PROMOTED ROUND 6]

A short, quotable sentence that closes a paragraph or follows a heading and compresses the point into a slogan, an abstraction, or a metaphor. It sounds decisive. The reader has to decode it, and often there's nothing underneath.

> Round 5 (JB Clark): *"[[Leading with AI is the new leading with features.]]"* cut; *"your buyer is looking at one claim with five price tags"* replaced with the plain consequence (*"your buyer doesn't see any differentiation in your service. At that point, they're shopping on price."*).
>
> Round 6 (Susan Ishmael), four instances in one draft:
> - *"That time belongs to the argument."*: *"I mean, I hate this sentence. It doesn't belong to the argument, it belongs to people. How about 'instead of asking your editors to work harder, create a better system that will eliminate unnecessary steps for your team.'"*
> - *"For those, a ceiling holds better than a ban."*: *"I'd eliminate this sentence."*
> - *"A ceiling only works if someone counts, so count."*: replaced with the checklist/"before times" passage quoted under Rule 10.
> - *"Notes become rules at different speeds."*: *"? makes no sense. I'd eliminate, and just start the paragraph with the next sentence."*

Two reviewers, two pieces, six instances, and neither reviewer saw the other's notes. That clears the bar. Rule 9 (restatement) and the main guide's figurative-language rule each caught part of this, but neither names the form. Susan's strongest note explains what's wrong with it: the line hands the point to an abstraction ("the argument") when the reader cares about **people** (editors, the team) and what they do.

**Rule:** after drafting, read every short sentence that closes a paragraph or follows a heading. For each one:
1. If it restates the paragraph or heading, cut it (Rule 9).
2. If it makes the point through an abstraction or an image, rewrite it as a plain statement of who does what. Name the people (editors, the team, the CISO) and the action.
3. If nothing concrete turns up underneath, cut it and let the paragraph end on its last working sentence.

Keep one deliberate punchline per piece at most, and only where it carries a real claim. This counts as the first CW instance of the imported candidate "Ground the abstract noun, or cut it." That candidate is now absorbed here.

### 12. In reader-facing copy, call them "editors," not "reviewers." Keep internal process vocabulary out of published copy. [NEW, ROUND 6 — terminology decision]

> Susan Ishmael changed "reviewers" to "editors" at every instance in the Round 6 draft: three margin comments reading *"editors,"* plus tracked changes at three more spots (including "an editor reviewer" → "an editor").
>
> Susan Ishmael, general comment: *"For example in this piece I don't like the words 'reviewer' 'ceiling' and 'argument.'"*

This is a terminology decision applied to every instance, so it promotes on one piece (see Process Notes on terminology). **Rule:** in published copy, the people who check drafts are **editors**. "Reviewer" stays acceptable inside this addendum, where it names the specific people giving feedback.

**Diagnosis (editor's note):** every word Susan flagged in Round 6 (*reviewer*, *ceiling*, *argument*, *ships*) is shop vocabulary from this addendum and the project instructions. The piece was about our own editorial process, and the drafting model carried our internal shorthand straight into public copy. **Broader rule:** when a piece describes Content Workshop's own process, translate internal terms into the words a reader would use. Watch for terms from this file in particular: ceiling, find-pass, two-instance bar, ship, promote, Watch List. *Ceiling*, *argument*, and *ship* are on the Watch List as single-reviewer word preferences.

## Watch List (first instance — need a second before promoting)

**Give named frameworks and models a distinctive, brandable name — not just a descriptive one.** *(Round 4. No framework-naming instance in Round 5 or Round 6; still pending.)*

> JB Clark, on the Timely/Painful/Yours framework: *"I like this framing but I want it to be a little more fun, like the circle of truth, interest circle. I don't know."* David Ebner: *"Agreed. I'm down for whatever you settle on."*

One instance, both active reviewers on the piece. When a blog introduces a proprietary framework, model, or named concept (a three-part test, a matrix, a funnel), draft two or three candidate names — at least one purely descriptive, at least one with a hook or wordplay — and flag both for the writer/reviewer to choose from, rather than defaulting straight to the descriptive option. Watching for whether this recurs on the next framework-driven piece before folding it into the main style guide's structure section.

**Avoid framing a common mistake as something "most teams" or "most marketers" get wrong; describe the failure mode instead of naming the audience as the ones who fail.** *(Round 4. No new instance in Rounds 5–6.)*

> David Ebner, on the heading "Where Most Teams Get This Wrong": *"Maybe 'Where it's easy to get this wrong.' It removes the finger-pointing aspect in 'most teams.'"*

One instance, but it connects directly to an existing, non-negotiable system instruction: never imply that the audience — sophisticated, time-pressed marketing and cybersecurity professionals — lacks understanding. Watching for whether this recurs in future headline drafts before folding it into the persona/tone section explicitly.

**Prefer "infer" over "predict" when describing how an AI model generates output.** *(Round 4. No instance in Rounds 5–6.)*

> David Ebner, on "AI agents don't reason the way a person does. They predict": *"I'd actually say 'infer' here."*

One instance, one reviewer, and genuinely debatable on technical grounds. Logging it rather than adopting it — needs a second instance or an explicit team decision before it becomes a word-choice rule.

**"Silent" and "quiet" are overused enough to flag as tell-words, not full clichés.** *(Round 3. Round 5 draft used neither. Round 6 used them only in quotation marks, as examples of tells; no flag.)*

> JB Clark, on "silent rewrite": *"Silent/quiet is another one of those Claude tingle words. It's not bad, I just think it's getting used A LOT."* Christina Rowell, same thread: *"This should be added to the list of words we don't want to do."*

One comment thread, one piece — but Christina's comment is an explicit instruction to add it to the list. High-confidence: the next piece that reaches for "silent" or "quiet" as a modifier should trigger an automatic self-check, and if it recurs, it promotes immediately rather than waiting for a third instance. Now on the Editor Checklist's find list.

**"Ship" / "ships" / "shipped" is a tell-word in reader-facing copy.** [NEW, ROUND 6 — high-confidence]

> Susan Ishmael, on *"...check the new wording against the list before it ships"*: *"'ships' is an AI tell to me. I'd say 'before you hit "publish".'"*

One instance, one reviewer, but it's an explicit "this is an AI tell" call, the same signal that made "silent/quiet" high-confidence. It's also heavily used in this addendum (see Rule 12's diagnosis), which makes it more likely to leak again. **Interim instruction:** on the find list; replace with "publish" or "hit publish" in copy. Promotes immediately on the next instance.

**"Ceiling" and "argument" as reader-facing words.** [NEW, ROUND 6 — high-confidence]

> Susan Ishmael: *"I don't like the words 'reviewer' 'ceiling' and 'argument.'"* On *"...more time for the subtle ones and for the argument"*: *"the use of the word 'argument' stops me here. I'd say '...and to edit the piece for clarity, focus, and message.'"*

The explicit word-list request makes this high-confidence. "Reviewer" promoted as a terminology decision (Rule 12). These two are word preferences, so they wait for a second instance. **Interim instruction:** on the find list. Use "limit" for *ceiling*. For *argument*, use "message," "point," or name what the editor actually does to the piece (clarity, focus, message). "Argument" stays fine inside this addendum and the project instructions.

**Turn an inline series of parallel fragments into a numbered or bulleted list.** [NEW, ROUND 6]

> Round 6 tracked changes (no comment): *"...so the editors built a guide to the habits instead: inflated claims of importance, vague trailing clauses, and prose that reads like a travel ad"* became a three-item numbered list, with each fragment capitalized and the commas and "and" removed.

One instance, no comment, so the reason is inferred: three named items read faster stacked than strung together. This is the reverse of Rule 7: fragments go in a list, and full sentences go in H3 + paragraph. **Interim guidance:** when a sentence lists three or more parallel fragments that the reader will want to scan, consider a short list.
